# Week 5: Who Are You Making This For? Audience and User Research

## 1. Introduction

ClaimTrail is designed to help non-specialist internet users investigate how online claims develop across different sources and languages. This week's focus is understanding the different people who could benefit from the project and how their needs should influence its design.

An important consideration is the false-consensus effect: the tendency to assume that other people share our own experiences, knowledge and opinions. Ross, Greene and House (1977) demonstrate how individuals can overestimate the extent to which others share their own attitudes and behaviours. As a designer, I need to recognise that my own approach to finding and evaluating information may not reflect the experiences of my intended audience. Therefore, user needs should inform the design rather than relying entirely on my assumptions.

## 2. Beneficiaries and Stakeholders

**Primary beneficiaries**

The primary audience is multilingual everyday internet users, particularly people who consume information in more than one language or use English as an additional language. They may encounter different versions of the same claim across news websites, social media and messaging platforms. ClaimTrail aims to help them investigate the origins, development and supporting evidence behind these claims.

**Secondary stakeholders**

- Journalists and fact-checkers who investigate the circulation of online information.
- Educators and media literacy organisations supporting critical information evaluation.
- Researchers studying misinformation, language and digital communication.
- Online communities that share information across linguistic boundaries.

**Excluded or underserved users**

People with limited digital literacy, visual impairments, limited internet access or whose languages receive weaker support from multilingual AI systems may experience barriers when using ClaimTrail. Keates, Clarkson and Robinson (2002) highlight the importance of considering users with different capabilities throughout the interface design process. This reinforces the need for accessible navigation, clear explanations and broader language testing.

## 3. User Personas

### Persona 1: Neyhan, 21 – The Everyday Digital User

Neyhan is a university student who regularly uses TikTok, Instagram and online news platforms. She frequently encounters information through social media and sometimes comes across claims that are difficult to verify. Although comfortable using digital platforms, she does not necessarily have the time or knowledge to investigate the original sources behind every claim.

**Needs:** Quick access to information, understandable explanations and direct links to supporting sources.

**Design implication:** ClaimTrail should offer a straightforward browser-based experience that makes investigating a claim simple and accessible.

### Persona 2: Mina, 29 – The Multilingual Information Seeker

Mina lives in the UK and speaks Bengali as her first language, with English as an additional language. She regularly reads English news and Bengali content shared through social media and family networks. When similar information appears in both languages, she sometimes finds that the wording or meaning differs.

**Needs:** Cross-language claim comparison, understandable explanations and visibility of how information changes between sources.

**Design implication:** ClaimTrail should support multilingual retrieval, translation-aware comparison and explanations in the user's preferred language.

### Persona 3: Shuily, 47 – The Non-Specialist Information User

Shuily is a working adult who regularly uses WhatsApp, Facebook and online news to keep informed and communicate with family and friends. She is comfortable with everyday technology but does not have specialist knowledge of artificial intelligence or information verification. She prefers clear explanations rather than complicated technical terminology.

**Needs:** An intuitive interface, accessible language and clear evidence that helps her assess information independently.

**Design implication:** ClaimTrail should avoid unnecessary technical terminology and present evidence through simple visual explanations.

## 4. User Research and Methodology

To understand the intended audience more closely, I conducted three semi-structured interviews with Neyhan, Mina and Shuily via online video chat. I selected participants with different ages, backgrounds and approaches to consuming online information to explore a range of user perspectives.

The interviews focused on participants' existing information habits, experiences of encountering conflicting claims and approaches to checking sources. I used the following key questions:

1. Where do you usually encounter information or news online?
2. What do you normally do when you come across information you are unsure about?
3. Have you encountered different versions of the same claim across different websites or languages?
4. How do you decide whether an online source is trustworthy?
5. What difficulties, if any, do you experience when checking information?
6. What would help you understand where an online claim originated?

Semi-structured interviews allowed me to explore consistent topics while giving participants the freedom to describe their own experiences. Following the interviews, I plan to organise their responses using thematic analysis, identifying recurring patterns and differences in their experiences. Braun and Clarke (2006) describe thematic analysis as a flexible method for identifying and interpreting patterns within qualitative data.

I chose purposive sampling because the participants were selected to reflect different characteristics relevant to the intended audience. For further research, I plan to recruit additional participants who regularly consume information across multiple languages.

## 5. Research Ethics

The interviews were conducted with consideration for informed consent, voluntary participation and respect for participants' privacy. Personal information should be minimised, and any findings shared in the project should be anonymised where appropriate.

Questions about misinformation should avoid making participants feel judged for previously believing or sharing inaccurate information. The research should focus on understanding information behaviours rather than testing participants' intelligence or digital competence. Further participant recruitment and data collection will follow the university's research ethics requirements.

## 6. Reflection and Next Steps

Developing these personas and conducting initial interviews has helped me recognise that ClaimTrail needs to accommodate different levels of digital confidence, language proficiency and information-seeking behaviour.

The three participants represent different perspectives on how people encounter and evaluate online information. Their experiences provide a starting point for identifying user needs and informing early design decisions. The research has also reinforced the importance of designing an accessible interface that accommodates different user capabilities rather than assuming everyone interacts with technology in the same way.

My next step is to review the interview responses, identify recurring themes and use these findings to inform the extension's interface, multilingual features and evidence visualisation. Ultimately, I want the design to support users' ability to evaluate information independently rather than simply asking them to trust an AI-generated conclusion.
